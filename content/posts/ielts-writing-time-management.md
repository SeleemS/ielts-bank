  <p>The <strong>IELTS Writing test</strong> gives you 60 minutes to complete two tasks, and how you divide that hour matters almost as much as what you write. Most candidates do not lose marks because their English is weak; they lose marks because they spend too long on Task 1, then rush or fail to finish Task 2 — the task that is worth twice as much. This guide gives you a clear 60-minute plan and the habits that keep you on schedule.</p>

  <h2>The format and the marks</h2>
  <p>Both the Academic and General Training Writing tests last <strong>60 minutes</strong> and contain two tasks:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Task 1</strong> — write at least <strong>150 words</strong>. Academic candidates describe a chart, graph, table, map or process; General Training candidates write a letter.</li>
    <li><strong>Task 2</strong> — write at least <strong>250 words</strong>. Both versions write an essay responding to a point of view, argument or problem.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>The single most important fact for pacing: <strong>Task 2 is worth twice as many marks as Task 1</strong>. That is why the standard, examiner-recommended split is <strong>20 minutes for Task 1 and 40 minutes for Task 2</strong>. Both tasks are marked on the same four criteria — Task Achievement or Task Response, Coherence and Cohesion, Lexical Resource, and Grammatical Range and Accuracy — and there is <strong>no separate time to transfer your answers</strong>, so everything happens inside the hour.</p>

  <h2>Your minute-by-minute plan</h2>
  <p>Here is a plan you can rehearse until it becomes automatic.</p>
  <h3>Task 1 — 20 minutes</h3>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Minutes 0–3: Analyse and plan.</strong> Identify what the visual (or letter prompt) is asking. For Academic, find the biggest trends, the highest and lowest values, and any obvious groupings. For General, note the three bullet points you must cover and the correct tone.</li>
    <li><strong>Minutes 3–17: Write.</strong> Open with a paraphrase of the prompt, give a clear overview of the main features (or your reason for writing), then two body paragraphs with specific data or details.</li>
    <li><strong>Minutes 17–20: Check.</strong> Confirm you are over 150 words and scan for missing articles, plurals and verb tenses.</li>
  </ol>
  <h3>Task 2 — 40 minutes</h3>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Minutes 0–5: Plan.</strong> Decide your position, then jot two main ideas with an example for each. Five minutes of planning saves you from a rambling, off-topic essay.</li>
    <li><strong>Minutes 5–35: Write.</strong> Introduction, two body paragraphs (one idea each, fully developed), and a conclusion that restates your position.</li>
    <li><strong>Minutes 35–40: Check.</strong> Confirm you are over 250 words and re-read for grammar and linking.</li>
  </ol>

  <h2>Should you write Task 2 first?</h2>
  <p>You may attempt the tasks in any order, and many high scorers write <strong>Task 2 first</strong>. The logic is simple: the more valuable task should get your freshest thinking and its full 40 minutes, before fatigue sets in. If you tackle Task 1 first and it overruns, the damage lands on the task that counts double. Whichever order you choose, <strong>set a hard limit on Task 1</strong> and stick to it.</p>

  <h2>Common time-management mistakes</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Over-writing Task 1.</strong> There is no reward for a 250-word Task 1; the extra words simply steal minutes from Task 2. Aim for roughly 160–190 words and stop.</li>
    <li><strong>Skipping the plan.</strong> Writing without a plan feels faster but usually produces a weaker, less coherent answer that costs marks under Coherence and Cohesion.</li>
    <li><strong>Ignoring the word count.</strong> Under-length answers are penalised, so learn how much of your own handwriting makes 150 and 250 words. See our guide on <a href="/blog/ielts-writing-word-count-minimums">word count minimums</a>.</li>
    <li><strong>No checking time.</strong> The last few minutes of each task catch the easy errors — articles, plurals, subject-verb agreement — that quietly cap essays at Band 6.</li>
  </ul>
