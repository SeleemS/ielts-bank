  <p>A word on honesty first: these are our own original essays, labelled with an <em>estimated</em> band. We do not reproduce official Cambridge material, and no examiner has marked these — the analysis is examiner-style, applied criterion by criterion against the public band descriptors.</p>

  <h2>Band 8 vs Band 9: the differences are small and consistent</h2>
  <p>Band 9 is not a different kind of writing from Band 8. It is Band 8 with the last weak links removed. Use this table as a checklist against your own essays.</p>
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th>Criterion</th><th>Band 8</th><th>Band 9</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr>
        <td><strong>Task Response</strong></td>
        <td>Position clear and developed; the occasional claim asserted rather than shown</td>
        <td>Every claim in the argument is supported or illustrated; nothing is left as a generalisation</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <td><strong>Coherence &amp; Cohesion</strong></td>
        <td>Logical, well-paragraphed; cohesion managed well</td>

      </tr>

  <p>Notice that none of the Band 9 column is about longer essays or rarer words. It is about closing gaps. Now let us see it on the page.</p>

  <h2>Sample 1 — Technology (positive or negative development)</h2>
  <p>This is one of the most common Task 2 frames. Try it yourself first on the <a href="/writingquestion/ielts-writing-task-2-the-automation-of-factory-work-xk3zzs">automation of factory work practice page</a> before you read our answer.</p>
  <blockquote>
    <p><em>Robots and automated machines are carrying out an increasing share of the work in factories that was once done by human hands. Is this increasing automation of manufacturing a positive or negative development?</em></p>
  </blockquote>
  <h3>Band 9 sample answer (written by our team)</h3>
  <p>The migration of factory work from human hands to robotic arms provokes real anxiety, and among the workers whose jobs are most exposed that anxiety is entirely justified. On balance, however, I regard the automation of manufacturing as a positive development, on the clear condition that its benefits are distributed rather than hoarded.</p>
  <p>The first case for it is economic. Because industrial robots neither tire nor lose concentration, they hold output to a consistent standard around the clock, driving down the cost of each unit produced; in genuinely competitive markets those savings are forced onto shelves as lower prices, because a rival that refuses to pass them on is simply undercut. Automation also lets high-wage economies keep production at home rather than exporting it, which is why Germany and South Korea remain manufacturing powers: their robot density allows domestic plants to compete with far cheaper labour abroad.</p>
  <p>The second, and to my mind weightier, benefit concerns the character of the work itself. The tasks machines take over first are the ones that wear bodies out — lifting, stamping, handling toxic substances — so the roles that survive, in maintenance, programming and supervision, tend to be both safer and more skilled.</p>
  <p>The obvious objection is that automation strips people of their livelihoods, and it deserves an honest answer rather than a dismissal. History, though, is instructive. When mechanised ploughs and combine harvesters spread across farms in the last century, agricultural employment collapsed, yet the displaced did not vanish into permanent idleness — they moved into the very factories and offices that industrialisation had opened up. The lesson is that technology redistributes work more than it abolishes it, and that the real damage is done not by the machine but by leaving the workforce to adjust unaided. Retraining programmes, transitional income and investment in affected regions are therefore essential.</p>
  <p>In short, automation makes goods cheaper and work safer, and its disruption is a problem for policy to solve, not a reason to halt progress.</p>

  <h3>Why this reaches Band 9</h3>
  <p>Compare it with the <a href="/ielts-essay-bank/automation-of-factory-work-band-8">Band 8 version of the same prompt</a> in our essay bank. That answer notes, in its own "Next band" feedback, three things it would need to reach 9 — and this sample does exactly those:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Task Response.</strong> The Band 8 essay <em>states</em> that technology "reshapes employment more than it eliminates it." The Band 9 version <em>illustrates</em> it, with the concrete shift from farm labour to factory and office work. No claim in the counter-argument is left as a bare generalisation.</li>
    <li><strong>Grammatical Range &amp; Accuracy.</strong> The Band 8 answer contained one agreement slip ("retraining … is essential"). Here the parallel list correctly takes a plural verb ("Retraining programmes, transitional income and investment … are essential"), and the range stays wide: a semicolon linking claim to evidence, a "not … but" contrast, and a fronted "History, though, is instructive."</li>
    <li><strong>Lexical Resource.</strong> The key idea is carried by varied nouns — <em>workers, the displaced, the workforce, people</em> — so the vocabulary feels as wide as the grammar, closing the one gap the Band 8 essay's feedback flagged.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Sample 2 — Education (agree or disagree)</h2>
  <p>A different question type — a to-what-extent opinion essay. Practise it on the <a href="/writingquestion/ielts-writing-task-2-should-university-education-be-free-for-everyone-p5ttxn">free university education practice page</a>.</p>
  <blockquote>
    <p><em>In some countries, students must pay high tuition fees to attend university, while others believe that governments should make higher education free for every qualified student. To what extent do you agree or disagree that university education should be free for all?</em></p>
  </blockquote>
  <h3>Band 9 sample answer (written by our team)</h3>
  <p>Whether the state should meet the full cost of a university education, or students should pay their own way through tuition fees, is ultimately a question of who benefits and who should therefore pay. My own view is that I largely agree that higher education should be free for every qualified student, though I would attach one important qualification.</p>
  <p>The strongest argument for abolishing fees is that a degree is not a purely private purchase; society as a whole draws on it. Doctors, engineers and teachers are all produced by universities, and a country starved of them pays far more — in poor health, weak infrastructure and stalled growth — than it would ever spend on tuition. Removing fees also widens the pool from which such graduates emerge: once the price of entry disappears, a gifted student from a low-income household is no longer deterred by the prospect of years of debt, and the nation keeps talent it would otherwise have wasted.</p>
  <p>There is, admittedly, a serious objection on grounds of cost. Free tuition is expensive, and if it is funded from general taxation, cleaners and shop assistants who never set foot in a lecture hall end up subsidising the future lawyers and bankers who will go on to out-earn them by a wide margin. This is a genuine unfairness, and it is why I cannot support making education free unconditionally.</p>
  <p>The resolution, to my mind, is to fund tuition publicly but to recover it through the tax system later, so that graduates who move into high salaries repay the cost through higher income tax while those who enter modestly paid but socially valuable professions, such as nursing, are spared. Access is thereby protected at the point of entry, where it matters most, and the eventual burden falls on those best able to bear it.</p>
  <p>In conclusion, university education should be free to enter, because its benefits are shared so widely, provided that the cost is later recovered fairly from those who profit most from it.</p>

  <h3>Why this reaches Band 9</h3>
  <p>Set it beside the <a href="/ielts-essay-bank/free-university-education-band-8">Band 8 free-education sample</a>. Three habits do the work:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>A position that is genuinely nuanced, not hedged.</strong> "Largely agree… with one qualification" is answered in full: the essay defends free access, then concedes the fairness objection, then <em>resolves</em> it with a specific mechanism (repayment through income tax). A Band 8 essay often states the objection; a Band 9 essay defeats it.</li>
    <li><strong>Every claim is grounded.</strong> The public benefit is named (doctors, engineers, teachers), the cost objection is made concrete (cleaners subsidising future lawyers), and the solution is specific rather than a vague call for "balance."</li>
    <li><strong>Range with control.</strong> Semicolons, a dash-framed aside, "once… ," and "provided that…" give variety, and the essay is effectively error-free — the two conditions Band 9 grammar requires.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>Reading models is necessary but not sufficient. This routine is what moves the score:</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Write a full timed answer first</strong>, on the practice page for the prompt, before you read our sample. You cannot learn the gap you never attempted.</li>
    <li><strong>Score yourself one criterion at a time</strong> against the Band 8-vs-9 table above, and for every claim ask a single question: <em>is this illustrated, or merely asserted?</em> That question alone accounts for much of the Task Response gap.</li>
    <li><strong>Fix only your weakest criterion.</strong> Your overall band is the average of four, so a lone 6 among three 8s is what caps you.
