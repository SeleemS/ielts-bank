  <p>Every IELTS examiner has read hundreds of essays with flawless grammar and impressive vocabulary that still scored <strong>Band 5</strong>. The reason is almost always the same: the candidate did not answer the question that was actually asked. In <strong>IELTS Writing Task 2</strong>, relevance is not a bonus &mdash; it is one of the four things you are marked on, and getting it wrong caps your score no matter how polished your English is. This guide shows you exactly how essays drift off-topic and how to keep yours locked onto the prompt from the first sentence to the last.</p>

  <h2>Why off-topic writing is so costly</h2>
  <p>Task 2 is scored on four equally weighted criteria: <strong>Task Response</strong>, <strong>Coherence and Cohesion</strong>, <strong>Lexical Resource</strong>, and <strong>Grammatical Range and Accuracy</strong>. Each contributes 25% of your Writing Task 2 mark. Task Response measures whether you answered the specific question, addressed every part of it, and supported a clear position. When you write about the general topic instead of the exact question, Task Response drops to around Band 5 &mdash; and because the four criteria are averaged, a Band 5 there drags a Band 8 grammar score down to roughly Band 6.5. You cannot &ldquo;write your way out&rdquo; of an off-topic answer with better vocabulary.</p>

  <h2>The three ways essays go off-topic</h2>
  <p>Drift almost always comes from one of three mistakes. Learn to recognise them:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Answering the topic, not the question.</strong> The prompt mentions &ldquo;online education,&rdquo; so the candidate writes everything they know about online education &mdash; instead of answering the precise question, which might be whether its <em>benefits outweigh its drawbacks</em>.</li>
    <li><strong>Ignoring the task instruction.</strong> The question says &ldquo;discuss both views and give your own opinion,&rdquo; but the candidate only argues one side. Half the task is missing, so Task Response is capped.</li>
    <li><strong>Drifting mid-essay.</strong> The introduction is on point, but a body paragraph wanders into a loosely related idea &mdash; a personal anecdote, a tangent about technology in general &mdash; that never links back to the thesis.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Step 1: Decode the exact question before you write</h2>
  <p>Read the prompt twice and underline two things: the <strong>keywords</strong> (the specific topic and any limits on it) and the <strong>task instruction</strong> (what you must do with that topic). Consider this prompt:</p>
  <p><em>&ldquo;Some people believe that children should be taught to compete, while others think they should be taught to cooperate. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.&rdquo;</em></p>
  <p>The keywords are <strong>children</strong>, <strong>compete</strong>, and <strong>cooperate</strong> &mdash; not education in general, not adults in the workplace. The instruction is <strong>discuss both views and give your own opinion</strong>, which means all three obligations must appear: the case for competition, the case for cooperation, and your stance. Miss any one and you have only partly answered.</p>

  <h2>Step 2: Answer the precise question, not the general theme</h2>
  <p>Once you know the keywords and instruction, write a one-line answer to the actual question &mdash; that becomes your <strong>thesis</strong>. For the prompt above, a focused thesis is: <em>&ldquo;While competition builds resilience, I believe cooperation should be prioritised because it develops the teamwork children rely on for life.&rdquo;</em> Notice it names both views and states a position. If your thesis could sit on top of a dozen different essays about children, it is too general &mdash; sharpen it until it answers <em>this</em> question only.</p>

  <h2>Step 3: Keep every paragraph tied to the prompt</h2>
  <p>Off-topic drift happens sentence by sentence, so build in checkpoints. Start each body paragraph with a <strong>topic sentence that echoes a keyword</strong> from the question, and end each paragraph by asking yourself: <em>does this still answer what was asked?</em> A reliable structure keeps you honest:</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Introduction:</strong> paraphrase the question and state your thesis.</li>
    <li><strong>Body 1:</strong> the first view, explained and supported, tied back to the keywords.</li>
    <li><strong>Body 2:</strong> the second view, plus why you favour your position.</li>
    <li><strong>Conclusion:</strong> restate your opinion in answer to the exact question.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>Examples are where drift is most tempting. A concrete example is good, but it must illustrate the point &mdash; if a story about your own school days does not directly support your argument about competition versus cooperation, cut it.</p>

  <h2>A quick relevance checklist</h2>
  <ul>
    <li>Did I underline the keywords and the task instruction before writing?</li>
    <li>Does my thesis answer <strong>this</strong> question and no other?</li>
    <li>If the task has two parts, does my plan address both?</li>
    <li>Does every topic sentence connect to a keyword from the prompt?</li>
    <li>Does my conclusion restate my position on the exact question asked?</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>What to do if you realise you have drifted</h2>
  <p>If you notice halfway through that you have misread the question, do not panic or scrap everything &mdash; you rarely have time. Add a sentence that pivots back to the real question, adjust your next paragraph and conclusion to address it, and make your position unmistakable. A partially recovered essay still earns more Task Response credit than one that ignores the prompt entirely.</p>

  <p>Relevance is a habit you can drill. Take real prompts from our <a href="/writingquestion">IELTS Writing Questions</a> page and, for each, write only the thesis and four topic sentences &mdash; no full essay &mdash; then check that each one echoes a keyword. Do ten of these and decoding any prompt becomes automatic.   <p>Strong English is not enough on its own. Decode the exact question, answer it directly, and keep every paragraph tied to the prompt &mdash; a relevant Band 6 essay will always beat an impressive one that answers the wrong question.</p>
