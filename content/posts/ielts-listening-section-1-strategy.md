  <p>Part 1 of <strong>IELTS Listening</strong> is the section most likely to give you full marks — and the section where careless mistakes hurt the most. The test has <strong>four parts, 40 questions and about 30 minutes of audio</strong>, and the recording plays <strong>once only</strong>. Section 1 is questions <strong>1 to 10</strong>: a conversation between <strong>two people in an everyday, social context</strong>, such as a caller booking a holiday cottage, a student enquiring about a language course, or someone registering for a local club. The topics are familiar, the pace is manageable, and the answers are almost always concrete details. That is exactly why you should treat it as a section to win outright, not to relax into.</p>

  <h2>What Section 1 actually tests</h2>
  <p>Unlike the academic discussion in Part 3 or the lecture in Part 4, Section 1 is deliberately practical. You are listening for information a real person would need to write down: names, addresses, phone numbers, dates, times, prices, and reference numbers. The dominant question type is <strong>form, note or table completion</strong>, where you fill gaps in a partly completed form as the conversation unfolds. Because the format is so consistent, Section 1 rewards preparation more than any other part of the test. For a deeper look at the mechanics of filling those gaps, see our guide to <a href="/blog/ielts-listening-form-note-completion">form and note completion</a>.</p>

  <h2>Use the preview time to predict every answer</h2>
  <p>Before each section, you are given time to read the questions. In Section 1 this is gold. Read every gap and ask what <em>kind</em> of word must fill it, because that tells your ear what to wait for:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>A gap after "Name:"</strong> means a name will be <em>spelled out</em> — get ready to write letters.</li>
    <li><strong>A gap after "Date:" or "Time:"</strong> means listen for a day, month or clock time in a specific format.</li>
    <li><strong>A gap with a currency symbol or "£" nearby</strong> means a price is coming — note whether it wants the number only.</li>
    <li><strong>A gap after "Postcode" or "Reference number"</strong> means a mix of letters and digits, often said one character at a time.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Predicting the answer type turns listening into confirming rather than searching. You already know a number is coming; you just have to catch which one.</p>

  <h2>Listen for corrections — the classic Section 1 trap</h2>
  <p>Section 1 loves to give you an answer and then change it. A speaker says a time, a date or a spelling, and a second later corrects it: <em>"So that's the 15th — oh, sorry, no, the 50th, I mean the 5th."</em> The <strong>final</strong> version is the answer. Signal words to watch for include <strong>"actually," "sorry," "I mean," "or rather," "on second thoughts,"</strong> and <strong>"let me change that."</strong> Never lock in the first number you hear; keep your pen ready to overwrite it. This single habit protects two or three marks in a typical Section 1.</p>

  <h2>Master spellings, numbers and the word limit</h2>
  <p>Here is the uncomfortable truth about Section 1: most lost marks are not listening failures at all. They are writing failures. A misspelled name, a missing plural "s," or a number written in the wrong format is marked <strong>wrong</strong>, even when you clearly heard it. Three rules protect you:</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Spelling must be exact.</strong> If a name or word is spelled out letter by letter, copy it precisely. If it is a common word, standard spelling is required — both British and American spellings are accepted, but a typo is not.</li>
    <li><strong>Watch singular versus plural.</strong> "Ticket" and "tickets" are different answers. Let grammar in the sentence guide you.</li>
    <li><strong>Obey the word limit.</strong> If the instruction says <strong>NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER</strong>, writing three words scores zero regardless of meaning.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>For the fine detail on how letters, dates, decimals and phone numbers are expected to appear on your answer sheet, read our dedicated guide to <a href="/blog/ielts-listening-spelling-numbers-word-limit-rules">spelling, numbers and word limits</a>.</p>

  <h2>Transferring your answers</h2>
  <p>On the <strong>paper-based</strong> test you get <strong>10 minutes at the end</strong> to copy all your answers onto the answer sheet — use it to double-check spellings and plurals, not just to transcribe. On the <strong>computer-delivered</strong> test your answers are already recorded, and you get about <strong>2 minutes</strong> at the end to review. Either way, revisit any Section 1 gap you rushed; it is the cheapest place to recover a mark.</p>

  <h2>A quick practice routine</h2>
  <p>Because Section 1 is so formulaic, targeted practice pays off fast. Do full Section 1 sets under timed conditions, then mark yourself strictly — count spelling and plural slips as wrong, exactly as the real test will. Keep an error log: if you keep dropping marks on numbers, drill numbers; if names catch you out, practise the alphabet spoken aloud. You can work through realistic sets on our <a href="/listeningquestion">Listening practice questions</a> page. With a little discipline, 10 out of 10 in Section 1 is a genuinely achievable target — and those ten marks lift your whole Listening band.</p>
