  <p>One of the most common fears IELTS candidates have about the <strong>Reading test</strong> is meeting a word they have never seen. It feels like a wall: if you do not know the word, how can you answer the question? The good news is that you almost never need to. IELTS Reading gives you <strong>40 questions in 60 minutes</strong> across three passages, and <strong>no dictionaries are allowed</strong>. The test measures whether you can find and understand the information a question asks about, not whether you have memorised every term. This guide shows you how to work out unfamiliar words from the text around them so a single unknown word never derails an answer.</p>

  <h2>First, Decide Whether the Word Even Matters</h2>
  <p>Before you spend any energy decoding a word, ask one question: <strong>does the answer depend on it?</strong> Often it does not. If an unknown word sits in a sentence that has nothing to do with the question you are working on, skip it and keep moving. Wasting 90 seconds on a word that never appears in the answer is one of the fastest ways to run out of time. Only slow down to decode a word when it is inside the sentence that holds your answer, or when it is one of the keywords you are scanning for.</p>

  <h2>Use the Sentence Around the Word</h2>
  <p>When a word does matter, the writer has usually left clues nearby. Train yourself to look for these four signals.</p>

  <h3>1. Definitions and restatements</h3>
  <p>Academic writing frequently explains its own terms. Watch for punctuation and phrases that introduce a definition: commas, dashes, brackets, or expressions like <em>"that is,"</em>, <em>"in other words,"</em>, <em>"known as"</em> and <em>"which means"</em>. In <em>"Coral bleaching, the loss of colour that occurs when algae leave the coral, is increasing"</em>, the phrase after the comma defines the term for you.</p>

  <h3>2. Examples</h3>
  <p>Signal words such as <em>"for example"</em>, <em>"such as"</em>, <em>"including"</em> and <em>"like"</em> point to examples that reveal meaning. If a passage says <em>"nocturnal animals, such as owls, bats and foxes, hunt after dark"</em>, you can infer that <strong>nocturnal</strong> relates to night, even if you have never met the word.</p>

  <h3>3. Contrast</h3>
  <p>Contrast words tell you a meaning is the opposite of something nearby. Watch for <em>"but"</em>, <em>"however"</em>, <em>"whereas"</em>, <em>"unlike"</em>, <em>"on the other hand"</em> and <em>"despite"</em>. In <em>"The city was once affluent, but decades of decline left it impoverished"</em>, the contrast tells you <strong>affluent</strong> is the opposite of poor, so it means wealthy.</p>

  <h3>4. Cause and effect</h3>
  <p>Words like <em>"because"</em>, <em>"so"</em>, <em>"as a result"</em> and <em>"therefore"</em> connect a cause to its consequence, and the half you understand explains the half you do not. If <em>"the drug proved efficacious, so doctors began prescribing it widely"</em>, the result tells you <strong>efficacious</strong> means effective.</p>

  <h2>Read the Word's Grammar and Its Parts</h2>
  <p>You can narrow a meaning even further by noticing how the word behaves. Is it a noun (a thing), a verb (an action) or an adjective (a description)? Its position in the sentence tells you. A word after "the" and before a verb is a noun; a word describing a noun is an adjective. Knowing the role stops you from guessing wildly.</p>
  <p>Word parts help too. Prefixes and suffixes carry meaning across thousands of words: <strong>un-</strong>, <strong>in-</strong>, <strong>dis-</strong> and <strong>mis-</strong> usually reverse or negate; <strong>-less</strong> means "without"; <strong>-able</strong> means "capable of"; <strong>re-</strong> means "again". A word like <em>"irreversible"</em> breaks down into <em>ir- (not) + reverse + -ible (able to be)</em>, which is enough to answer most questions.</p>

  <h2>Why This Matters More for Some Question Types</h2>
  <p>Guessing from context is especially important where the test deliberately paraphrases the passage. In <a href="/reading/true-false-not-given">True, False, Not Given</a> questions, a statement rarely repeats the passage word for word, so you must understand meaning rather than match vocabulary. The same is true for <a href="/reading/matching-headings">Matching Headings</a>, where the correct heading captures a paragraph's main idea in different words. If you only recognise identical words, the test's synonyms and rephrasing will trap you. You can review the full range of task types on the <a href="/readingquestion">Reading questions hub</a>.</p>

  <h2>A Quick Method You Can Practise</h2>
  <ol>
    <li>Reach an unknown word and check whether it affects your answer. If not, move on.</li>
    <li>If it does, read the whole sentence, then the sentence before and after.</li>
    <li>Look for a definition, an example, a contrast or a cause-and-effect clue.</li>
    <li>Identify the word's grammatical role and break it into parts.</li>
    <li>Make your best guess, plug it into the sentence, and check it makes sense.</li>
  </ol>

  <h2>Common Mistakes to Avoid</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Stopping dead at every hard word.</strong> Fluent readers glide past words that do not matter; anxious readers freeze. Keep your eyes moving.</li>
    <li><strong>Guessing from the word alone.</strong> A word out of context is a coin toss. The clues live in the sentences around it, so always read on.</li>
    <li><strong>Assuming a familiar-looking word means what it looks like.</strong> "Novel" can mean a book or mean "new". Let the context, not your first instinct, decide.</li>
    <li><strong>Ignoring pacing.</strong> Even good guessing takes seconds you do not have to spare, so build the habit alongside solid timing.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>You will not eliminate unknown words from the IELTS Reading test, and you do not need to. What separates higher scorers is not a bigger dictionary in their head but the calm habit of reading around a word, gathering its clues, and moving on. Practise it on every passage you do, and the wall of an unfamiliar word turns into a puzzle you can almost always solve.</p>
