  <p>Almost every candidate has the same nightmare about Part 2: you get the card, you talk for forty seconds, and then your mind goes blank while the examiner watches you in silence. That silence feels like the end of the world in the moment, but it is completely avoidable. The skill Part 2 really tests is not having interesting things to say — it is being able to <strong>keep talking fluently for up to two minutes</strong> about any topic you are handed. This guide shows you exactly how to stretch a thin idea into a full long turn.</p>

  <h2>First, know what Part 2 actually is</h2>
  <p>In Part 2, the examiner gives you a <strong>cue card</strong> with a topic and three or four bullet-point prompts, plus a sheet of paper and a pencil. You have <strong>one minute to prepare</strong> and then you speak on your own for <strong>one to two minutes</strong>. The examiner will stop you at two minutes — so your goal is to still be talking when they interrupt you, not to "finish" early. Part 2 is marked on the same four criteria as the rest of the test: Fluency and Coherence, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range and Accuracy, and Pronunciation. Notice that <strong>none of those criteria reward accurate facts</strong>. You are never judged on whether your story is true, only on the English you use to tell it.</p>

  <h2>Use the bullet prompts as a checklist, not a script</h2>
  <p>The prompts on the card exist precisely so you never run dry. Treat them as a route through your answer, and squeeze every one of them. A typical card such as "Describe a place you like to visit" will ask you to say where it is, how often you go, what you do there, and why you like it. Do not race through all four in thirty seconds — spend real time on each. During your preparation minute, jot one or two words next to each prompt so you have signposts to follow while you speak.</p>

  <h2>Six ways to expand any idea</h2>
  <p>When you feel yourself running out, do not reach for a new topic — go <em>deeper</em> into the one you have. These expansion moves work for any cue card:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Give reasons.</strong> Every statement can be followed by "because…". If you say you enjoy something, explain <em>why</em> for two or three sentences.</li>
    <li><strong>Add a specific example.</strong> "For instance, the last time I went…" turns a general claim into a mini-story that eats up time naturally.</li>
    <li><strong>Bring in the senses.</strong> Describe what you could see, hear, smell or feel. Sensory detail is easy to generate and shows off descriptive vocabulary.</li>
    <li><strong>Talk about feelings.</strong> Say how the experience made you feel and why it mattered to you — examiners love reflection, and it is endlessly extendable.</li>
    <li><strong>Compare.</strong> Contrast it with something else: "unlike other places I've been…" or "it's much better now than it used to be."</li>
    <li><strong>Shift the timeframe.</strong> Talk about the past, then the present, then the future — "I first went as a child… these days… and I'm hoping to go back next year."</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Invent freely — the content is not marked</h2>
  <p>This is the single most useful thing to remember: <strong>nothing you say is fact-checked</strong>. If the card asks about a book that changed your life and you rarely read, do not freeze — invent a book, an author and a reason. A detailed, confident, imaginary answer scores far higher than a true answer that collapses into silence. Prepare a few flexible "stories" you can bend onto many topics — a memorable trip, a person you admire, a skill you learned — and adapt them on the spot. You can see how this works in practice in our <a href="/blog/ielts-speaking-part-2-cue-card">full Part 2 cue card walkthrough</a>.</p>

  <h2>Handle the blank moment without stopping</h2>
  <p>If your mind does go blank mid-answer, the worst response is to stop and apologise. Instead, buy yourself time with a natural filler that also moves the answer forward: "That's an interesting thing to think about, actually…" or "Now that I mention it, another thing worth saying is…". These phrases sound fluent and give your brain a second to find the next idea. Avoid empty repetition and long "ummm" pauses — a short, purposeful phrase is fine, but dead silence is what damages your <a href="/blog/ielts-speaking-fluency-and-coherence">Fluency and Coherence score</a>.</p>

  <h2>Practise against the clock</h2>
  <p>The reason candidates dry up is almost always that they have never rehearsed speaking for a full two minutes. Fix that in preparation: pick a random cue card, take your one minute of notes, and record yourself talking until you hit two minutes. Play it back and mark exactly where you stalled — that is where you needed one more reason or example. Do this daily and the two-minute long turn stops feeling long at all. For the questions that follow in Part 3 and how to develop those answers, work through our <a href="/speakingquestion">IELTS Speaking practice questions</a> and the guide to <a href="/blog/ielts-speaking-part-3-discussion">the Part 3 discussion</a>.</p>

  <p>Running out of things to say is not a sign that your English is weak — it is a sign that you have not yet trained the specific habit of extending an answer. Master the expansion moves above, give yourself permission to invent, and treat the examiner's two-minute cut-off as the finish line you are aiming to reach. Do that, and the Part 2 silence you fear simply never arrives.</p>
