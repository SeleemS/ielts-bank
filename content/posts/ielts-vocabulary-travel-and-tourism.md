  <p>Travel and tourism is one of the topics you are almost certain to meet somewhere in <strong>IELTS</strong>. It appears in Speaking Part 1 (how you prefer to travel, whether you like holidays), in Part 2 cue cards about a place you have visited or a memorable trip, in Part 3 discussions about tourism and its effects, and in Writing Task 2 questions on whether tourism benefits local communities or damages them. The trap most candidates fall into is preparing with stiff brochure language — "picturesque destinations of unparalleled beauty" — that no fluent speaker uses in conversation. What the <strong>Lexical Resource</strong> criterion actually rewards is natural collocation: everyday words in the pairings real English uses. This guide gives you those pairings, grouped by sub-topic, with notes on using them accurately.</p>

  <h2>The principle: natural pairings over big words</h2>
  <p>A high vocabulary band comes from words that sit together naturally, not from rare words dropped in for show. "I had a relaxing holiday and came back feeling refreshed" scores better than "I experienced a rejuvenating sojourn," because the first sounds like a real person and the second sounds memorised. Aim for four or five natural collocations in an answer, placed where the meaning needs them, and let the rest of your language be clear and ordinary. Examiners are trained to notice memorised chunks, so quality and fit matter far more than quantity.</p>

  <h2>Journeys and getting there</h2>
  <p>These pairings cover the mechanics of travelling, which come up in Part 1 and in narrative Part 2 answers:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>go on a trip</strong> / <strong>take a trip</strong> — the natural verbs; you do not "make a trip."</li>
    <li><strong>set off early</strong> — to begin a journey: "We set off early to beat the traffic."</li>
    <li><strong>a long-haul flight</strong> and <strong>a direct flight</strong> — long-haul means many hours; a direct flight has no changes.</li>
    <li><strong>a stopover</strong> and <strong>jet lag</strong> — a stopover is a break between flights; jet lag is the tiredness from crossing time zones.</li>
    <li><strong>travel light</strong> / <strong>pack light</strong> — to take very little luggage.</li>
    <li><strong>book in advance</strong> — to reserve early, often to save money.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Types of trip and accommodation</h2>
  <p>These let you describe what kind of traveller you are, a common Part 1 and Part 3 theme:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>a package holiday</strong> — flights, hotel and sometimes meals booked together.</li>
    <li><strong>a city break</strong> — a short trip to a city, usually a weekend.</li>
    <li><strong>a road trip</strong> and <strong>go backpacking</strong> — driving a long route for pleasure; travelling cheaply with a rucksack.</li>
    <li><strong>go sightseeing</strong> and <strong>a guided tour</strong> — visiting famous places; a tour led by a guide.</li>
    <li><strong>off the beaten track</strong> — away from the usual tourist places.</li>
    <li><strong>a staycation</strong> — a holiday spent at or near home.</li>
    <li><strong>check in</strong> and <strong>check out</strong> — arriving at and leaving accommodation; a hotel can also be <strong>fully booked</strong>.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Describing places and experiences</h2>
  <p>Part 2 cue cards such as "describe a place you enjoyed visiting" reward vivid but natural description. These earn their place:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>a bustling city</strong> / <strong>a picturesque village</strong> — busy and lively; pretty in an old-fashioned way.</li>
    <li><strong>a breathtaking view</strong> — genuinely stunning; keep it for scenery that deserves it.</li>
    <li><strong>steeped in history</strong> — with a long, rich past: "The old town is steeped in history."</li>
    <li><strong>soak up the atmosphere</strong> — to enjoy the feeling of a place.</li>
    <li><strong>get away from it all</strong> and <strong>recharge my batteries</strong> — to escape routine and rest. Use at most one idiom like these per answer, and develop it properly rather than stacking several.</li>
    <li><strong>a once-in-a-lifetime trip</strong> — a rare, special experience.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Tourism as a Writing Task 2 topic</h2>
  <p>When tourism appears in an essay, you need the vocabulary of impacts, not description. Useful, accurate pairings include:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>boost the local economy</strong> and <strong>create jobs</strong> — the standard economic arguments in favour.</li>
    <li><strong>generate revenue</strong> / <strong>bring in foreign currency</strong> — money tourism brings to a country.</li>
    <li><strong>over-tourism</strong> and <strong>mass tourism</strong> — too many visitors; travel on a very large scale.</li>
    <li><strong>preserve cultural heritage</strong> versus <strong>erode local traditions</strong> — the cultural upside and downside.</li>
    <li><strong>sustainable tourism</strong> and <strong>ecotourism</strong> — travel that limits environmental damage.</li>
    <li><strong>seasonal work</strong> — jobs that exist only part of the year, a common drawback.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Common mistakes with travel vocabulary</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>"a travel" / "travels."</strong> <em>Travel</em> is uncountable. For one journey say <em>a trip</em>, <em>a journey</em> or <em>a holiday</em>: "I went on a trip," not "I made a travel."</li>
    <li><strong>"go to abroad."</strong> <em>Abroad</em> takes no preposition — <em>go abroad</em>, <em>study abroad</em>.</li>
    <li><strong>trip vs journey vs voyage.</strong> A <em>trip</em> is the whole visit (there and back); a <em>journey</em> is the travelling itself; a <em>voyage</em> is a long trip by sea or in space, so do not use it for a flight.</li>
    <li><strong>tourism vs tourist vs tour.</strong> <em>Tourism</em> is the industry, a <em>tourist</em> is a person, and a <em>tour</em> is an organised visit. "The tourism visited the city" is wrong.</li>
    <li><strong>"fun" as an adjective.</strong> "It was a very fun holiday" is informal; in Writing prefer <em>enjoyable</em> or <em>memorable</em>.</li>
  </ul>
