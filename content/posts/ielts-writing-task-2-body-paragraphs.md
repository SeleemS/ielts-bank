  <p>Most IELTS candidates know how to open and close a Task 2 essay, yet lose marks in the middle. The body paragraphs are where the essay is actually won: they carry the arguments, and they are where the examiner looks for real development of ideas. A body paragraph that states an opinion but never explains or proves it caps your Task Response — no matter how neat the introduction. This guide gives you a repeatable four-step method for building body paragraphs that develop and support every idea.</p>

  <h2>Why body paragraphs decide your band</h2>
  <p>In Writing Task 2 you write a discursive essay of <strong>at least 250 words</strong> in roughly <strong>40 minutes</strong>, marked on four equally weighted criteria: <strong>Task Response</strong>, <strong>Coherence and Cohesion</strong>, <strong>Lexical Resource</strong>, and <strong>Grammatical Range and Accuracy</strong>. The body paragraphs are where Task Response is truly tested. The descriptors reward ideas that are <em>extended and supported</em>, not merely mentioned. A common Band 5–6 pattern is listing three or four points without developing any of them; a Band 7 essay takes fewer ideas and explains each one properly.</p>
  <p>The practical takeaway: <strong>two well-developed body paragraphs beat three thin ones.</strong> Depth is the goal, not coverage.</p>

  <h2>The four-step paragraph method</h2>
  <p>Every strong body paragraph develops <strong>one</strong> main idea through the same four moves, in order:</p>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Point</strong> — a topic sentence stating the paragraph's main idea.</li>
    <li><strong>Explain</strong> — a sentence or two explaining <em>why</em> that idea is true.</li>
    <li><strong>Example</strong> — a specific illustration that proves it.</li>
    <li><strong>Link</strong> — a closing sentence tying the idea back to your position or the question.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>This is often shortened to <strong>PEEL</strong>. Learn it once and every body paragraph you ever write has a clear shape. Let's build one step by step.</p>

  <h3>Step 1: The topic sentence (Point)</h3>
  <p>Open with a single sentence that names the idea and nothing else. It signals to the examiner exactly what this paragraph will argue, which directly helps your Coherence and Cohesion. Keep it clean and specific.</p>
  <p>For the prompt <em>"Some people think governments should invest in public transport rather than new roads. To what extent do you agree?"</em>, a topic sentence might be: <em>"The strongest argument for prioritising public transport is its capacity to reduce urban congestion."</em> One idea, clearly stated.</p>

  <h3>Step 2: Explain the idea</h3>
  <p>This is the step most candidates skip, and it is the one that separates Band 6 from Band 7. Answer the question <em>why?</em> Do not jump straight to an example — first explain the reasoning that connects your point to your position.</p>
  <p>Continuing the paragraph: <em>"When a city expands its rail and bus networks, a single vehicle can carry the passengers of dozens of private cars, so the number of vehicles competing for road space falls. Building more roads, by contrast, tends to attract additional traffic until the new capacity is absorbed."</em> Notice this explains the mechanism rather than just asserting it.</p>

  <h3>Step 3: Give a specific example</h3>
  <p>An example makes the abstract concrete. It does <strong>not</strong> have to be a real statistic — examiners do not fact-check, and invented figures often read as unnatural. A specific, plausible scenario is safer and works just as well.</p>
  <p>For instance: <em>"A commuter who switches from driving to a reliable metro line removes one car from the morning rush entirely; multiplied across a large workforce, this is the difference between a gridlocked and a moving city."</em> Specific, vivid, and it clearly supports the point.</p>

  <h3>Step 4: Link back</h3>
  <p>Close the paragraph by connecting the idea to your overall answer. This stops each paragraph feeling like an isolated fact and keeps the essay coherent: <em>"For this reason, directing funding towards public transport addresses the congestion problem at its source in a way that new roads cannot."</em></p>

  <h2>A full body paragraph, assembled</h2>
  <p>Put the four steps together and you get a paragraph that develops one idea completely:</p>
  <p><em>"The strongest argument for prioritising public transport is its capacity to reduce urban congestion. When a city expands its rail and bus networks, a single vehicle can carry the passengers of dozens of private cars, so the number of vehicles competing for road space falls, whereas building more roads tends to attract additional traffic until the new capacity is absorbed. A commuter who switches from driving to a reliable metro line removes one car from the morning rush entirely; multiplied across a large workforce, this is the difference between a gridlocked and a moving city. For this reason, directing funding towards public transport addresses the congestion problem at its source in a way that new roads cannot."</em></p>
  <p>That is roughly five sentences developing a single idea — exactly what Task Response rewards. Your second body paragraph follows the identical pattern with a different point.   <h2>Common body-paragraph mistakes</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Two ideas in one paragraph.</strong> Each paragraph gets exactly one main idea. A second point needs its own paragraph or its own explanation, not a squeeze into the first.</li>
    <li><strong>Point with no explanation.</strong> Stating a reason and moving straight on ("Public transport is good because it helps the environment. Also...") caps your development mark. Always answer <em>why</em>.</li>
    <li><strong>Examples that repeat the point.</strong> An example should add new, concrete detail, not restate the topic sentence in different words.</li>
    <li><strong>No link back.</strong> Paragraphs that stop after the example feel unfinished. One closing sentence tying the idea to your position fixes this.</li>
    <li><strong>Memorised example banks.</strong> Pre-learned examples rarely fit the exact question and examiners are trained to spot them. Generate a fresh, specific illustration for the prompt in front of you.</li>
  </ul>
