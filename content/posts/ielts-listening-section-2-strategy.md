  <p>Section 2 of <strong>IELTS Listening</strong> is where the test changes character. The paper has <strong>four parts, 40 questions and about 30 minutes of audio</strong>, played <strong>once only</strong>. Sections 1 and 2 are set in everyday, social contexts; Sections 3 and 4 are academic. What makes Section 2 distinctive is that it is the <strong>first monologue</strong>: questions <strong>11 to 20</strong> are carried by a single speaker — a tour guide, an event organiser, a radio presenter describing a local facility. There is no second person to interrupt, confirm or repeat, so the information arrives in one continuous stream. That shift catches candidates who cruised through Section 1.</p>

  <h2>What Section 2 actually tests</h2>
  <p>The context stays practical — a talk about a leisure centre, a museum, a festival, a new housing development, or arrangements for a trip. But the question types are more varied than the form-filling of <a href="/blog/ielts-listening-section-1-strategy">Section 1</a>. In Section 2 you are very likely to meet <strong>map or plan labelling</strong>, <strong>matching</strong>, and <strong>multiple choice</strong>, often alongside a short run of sentence or note completion. Each of these demands a different kind of listening, so the first job is to identify which type each question set uses before the audio starts.</p>

  <h2>Guard the preview time</h2>
  <p>Before the recording plays, you are given time to read the questions. In Section 2 this is not optional warm-up — it is where the section is won or lost. With no dialogue to slow the pace, you cannot afford to read and listen at the same time. Use the preview to:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Identify the question type</strong> for each cluster, so you know whether you are matching, labelling a map, or choosing an option.</li>
    <li><strong>Read the map or plan</strong> if there is one — find the entrance, the compass, and any labelled landmark, so you can follow directions in real time.</li>
    <li><strong>Underline keywords</strong> in each question and predict likely paraphrases the speaker might use instead.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>The questions always follow the order of the talk, so knowing what is coming next lets you listen forward rather than scramble backward.</p>

  <h2>Map and plan labelling: anchor yourself first</h2>
  <p>Map labelling is the question type most associated with Section 2, and it panics candidates who have not practised it. The fix is to orient before the speaker moves. Locate your <strong>starting point</strong> — usually "the entrance" or "where we are now" — and the direction words on the diagram. Then track the speaker's language of movement: <em>"opposite," "next to," "just beyond," "on your left," "at the far end," "the second turning."</em> Do not try to hold the whole map in your head; move your pen along the plan as the speaker walks you through it. If you lose one label, leave it and lock onto the next landmark rather than freezing. Our worked guide to <a href="/blog/ielts-listening-map-labelling">map labelling</a> breaks this tracking method down step by step.</p>

  <h2>Matching and multiple choice: listen for the whole idea</h2>
  <p>Matching questions give you a list of items and a set of options to attach to each — for example, matching facilities to floors, or activities to days. The trap is that all the options are usually mentioned; only the <em>relationship</em> the question asks about is correct. Multiple choice works the same way: the speaker often refers to two or three of the options, and distractors are planted precisely to reward candidates who catch a single matching word instead of the meaning. Train yourself to wait for the <strong>complete statement</strong> before committing, because the sentence frequently turns — <em>"the pool was going to open in May, but that has now been pushed back to September."</em> For more on defeating those planted wrong answers, see our guide to <a href="/blog/ielts-listening-multiple-choice">multiple choice questions</a>.</p>

  <h2>Common Section 2 mistakes</h2>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Reading and listening at once.</strong> Because there is no pause from a second speaker, splitting your attention means missing the answer entirely. Finish previewing before the audio reaches that cluster.</li>
    <li><strong>Choosing on a keyword match.</strong> Distractors are built from the exact words in the question. Confirm the meaning, not the vocabulary.</li>
    <li><strong>Getting stranded on the map.</strong> One missed direction should not cost you the next three. Re-anchor on the following landmark.</li>
    <li><strong>Ignoring the word limit.</strong> On any completion answers, <strong>NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER</strong> means exactly that — a third word scores zero.</li>
  </ol>

  <h2>How to practise</h2>
  <p>Section 2 rewards exposure to its specific formats more than raw listening ability. Drill map-labelling sets until tracking directions feels automatic, and do matching and multiple-choice clusters where you deliberately note which distractors the recording used and why they were wrong. Keep an error log by question type, not just by score. You can work through realistic single-speaker sets on our <a href="/listeningquestion">Listening practice questions</a> page. Master the preview habit and the map-tracking method, and the first monologue stops being a step up in difficulty and becomes ten reliable marks.</p>
